Learn About PHG Scores

PHG scores have been built on the same principle PFF pioneered in football: grade every player on every play, not just the outcomes that show up in the box score. Every shot, hit, turnover, faceoff, and on-ice event contributes to a player's score. The result is a more complete picture of how a player actually performed, regardless of whether the bounces went their way.

What We Do

The PHG scoring system evaluates every skater on every play-by-play event in a game. We believe every shift counts, and that granular event-level data reveals things that traditional stats cannot. The system is built on grading contribution to production on a given play, not just the end result.

Did a player create a high-danger shot chance that the goalie robbed? That player contributed positively and receives credit for the quality of the opportunity, even though it didn't go in. Did a defenseman give the puck away in his own zone directly leading to a scoring chance? That turnover gets penalized regardless of whether the opposing team scored.

This approach eliminates luck from the equation. A goal scored on a weak shot from the perimeter receives less credit than a goal scored from in tight. A blocked shot on a high-danger chance is worth more than one blocked at the point. The grade reflects the actual impact of the play, not just whether it appeared on the scoresheet.

The Grading Scale

Each player receives a grade delta on every play-by-play event, with 0 representing a neutral or expected play. Most events fall within a -2.0 to +2.0 range. Goals are the exception. A goal bonus stacks on top of the shot delta, reflecting that scoring is the highest-impact play in hockey, the same way a touchdown carries outsized weight in football. A typical goal results in a combined delta of around +3.4. Raw deltas are accumulated across all events in a game, then normalized to a 0-100 scale where 60 = league average and one standard deviation equals 12 points.

Forwards and defensemen are normalized separately against their own position group, so a defenseman's score reflects how he performed relative to other defensemen, not forwards.

Zone and Situation Awareness

Not all plays of the same type are equal. PHG adjusts grade deltas based on where on the ice the event occurred and what the game situation was. A giveaway in your own defensive zone is graded far more harshly than one at the offensive blue line. A hit protecting your net carries more weight than a forecheck hit in the offensive zone.

The same logic applies to faceoffs. Winning a defensive zone draw while shorthanded is one of the most valuable plays a center can make, worth up to +1.20. Losing that same faceoff is penalized at -1.50. Powerplay faceoffs in the offensive zone are also amplified since losing one squanders the man advantage before it even starts.

Shorthanded situations apply a 1.5x multiplier to defensive plays like blocks, hits, and takeaways, reflecting the higher stakes of those actions while a team is killing a penalty.

Why Not Just Use Goals and Assists?

Traditional stats reward players who score, but hockey is a team sport where most players never lead the league in points. A shutdown defenseman who suppresses high-danger chances all game, kills penalties, and blocks shots contributes enormously without showing up in the box score. A fourth-line center who wins every key defensive-zone faceoff doesn't get credit in a goal column.

Points are also noisy. A player who scores on a 5% shooting night got lucky. A player who consistently creates high-danger chances but doesn't get the bounces deserves credit too. PHG measures the quality and impact of every action, not just whether the puck crossed the line.

The result: players who consistently do the right things grade out well even in low-scoring stretches, and players riding shooting luck or powerplay time don't get inflated scores they don't deserve.

Qualifications

Players must appear in at least 50% of their team's games to qualify for season rankings. Players below this threshold are still shown but excluded from rank calculations. Faceoff scores require a minimum of 10 faceoffs taken across the season.

Season grades are not simply an average of game grades. A player who posts consistent scores game after game will grade out higher than one who has a single outstanding night surrounded by poor performances. Sustained performance across a full season is harder to maintain and is reflected accordingly.
